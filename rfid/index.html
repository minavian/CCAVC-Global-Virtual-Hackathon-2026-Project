The whole trick in three steps

Power through the air, a switch, and a reader that listens to itself

1 Power by magnetism

The reader pushes electricity back and forth through a loop of wire, its coil, 13.56 million times a second. That makes an invisible magnetic field that keeps flipping direction.

The card has its own loop of wire hidden inside the plastic. When the flipping field passes through that loop, it pushes electricity around it. This is called induction, the same thing that charges an electric toothbrush on its stand.

—

2 Talking with a switch

To answer, the chip does not broadcast anything. It has no transmitter. It quickly connects and disconnects a small extra resistor across its loop, hundreds of thousands of times a second.

Think of two people holding a rope. One shakes it: that’s the reader. The other can’t shake (no battery!), but can grip a little tighter or looser. The person shaking can feel the difference in their own hand.

—

3 The reader listens to itself

Every flick of the card’s switch changes how hard the reader’s coil has to work, so the voltage on the reader’s own coil wobbles very slightly. The reader watches its own coil for those wobbles and turns them back into 1s and 0s, then into letters and numbers.

This is why the card needs no battery and no antenna for sending. The reader supplies the power and does all the “hearing”.

—

Deep dive 1 · Distance

Why you have to tap: the energy cliff

A magnet’s pull fades quickly as you move away, and the reader’s field is no different. Far from the coil, the field gets weaker with the cube of distance: twice as far means 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times weaker.

The voltage made in the card follows the field. The energy the chip gets goes with voltage squared, so twice as far means 8 × 8 = 64 times less energy. Close to the reader the coil’s own size softens this a bit. The chart shows the real, calculated curve.

—

Tilt and sliding sideways

Only the field that passes through the card’s loop counts, like rain falling into a bucket. Tilt the card and its opening faces the field less. At 90°, on its edge, almost no field gets through.

Sliding sideways hides a surprise. Past the edge of the reader’s coil, the field lines curl back around and point the other way. If part of the card’s loop sees the field going up and part sees it going down, the two cancel out. That leaves a dead spot with almost no energy, even quite close to the reader.

—

Deep dive 2 · Tuning

Pushing a swing at the right moment

A swing goes highest when you push it in time with its own natural rhythm. The card’s loop of wire, together with a tiny capacitor, has a natural rhythm too. Makers tune it to match the reader’s 13.56 million pushes per second.

When the rhythms match, each push adds to the last and the voltage builds up several times higher. This is called resonance. Mistune the card and the chip goes hungry.

—

Real cards are usually tuned a little higher, around 14–15 MHz. A hand, a wallet or a second card nearby drags the rhythm down, and starting a bit high leaves room for that.

Too close is strange. Bring the card right onto the reader and the single peak splits in two. The two loops are so tightly linked that they ring together as one system with two rhythms, and the reader’s 13.56 MHz falls in the valley between them. Physicists call this over-coupling. Here the chip still gets enough energy, but less than it would just a little farther away.

Deep dive 3 · The switch

Talking without a transmitter

Here is the card’s circuit. The coil catches the field, the capacitor tunes it, and the chip uses the energy. Next to the chip sits one more part: a switch with a resistor.

Closing the switch is like resting a finger on a ringing wine glass: the card’s “ringing” gets damped, and it soaks up the reader’s field differently. The reader’s coil feels that as a small change in its own voltage.

—

The trade-off engineers live with: a stronger switch makes a louder reply, but while the switch is closed the chip itself gets less energy. Near the edge of range, a very strong switch can starve the chip halfway through its answer. Try the strongest setting with the card far away.

Deep dive 4 · From letters to wobbles and back

How a message survives the trip

The chip can only do one thing, flip its switch, so everything it says must be turned into a pattern of flips. Type a message for the card to send. The reader will decode it from its own wobbling coil voltage, including the noise a real room adds.

1 · Letters become bytes, bytes become bits

Each letter is stored as a number (A = 65, B = 66 …), and that number is written in binary: eight 0s and 1s. They are sent smallest-bit first, as the standard says. A 9th check bit makes the number of 1s odd. A final check byte is built so that every column of bits has an even number of 1s. If one bit gets flipped on the way, a check fails and the reader asks again.

2 · What the switch does, what the reader sees, what it decodes

Top: the chip’s switch (pink = closed). Middle: the reader’s coil voltage, zoomed far in, noise and all. Bottom: the bits the reader decodes. Move your pointer or finger over the strip to magnify a few bits. Each bit lasts about 9.4 millionths of a second. A 1 means the switch flickers in the first half of the bit, a 0 in the second half. Every bit has a flicker somewhere, so the reader never loses count. Engineers call this Manchester coding.

Why flicker instead of a simple on/off? The reader’s own field is huge compared with the card’s reply, and it drifts slowly. A plain “switch closed for half a bit” reply looks just like that drift. When the chip flickers at a precise 847,500 times a second, the reader can listen for that one rhythm and ignore the rest, the way you can pick out a friend’s voice in a noisy room. Turn the flicker off and raise Slow wobble to see the difference. Notice that the plain method actually copes slightly better with pure random hiss. Engineering is full of trade-offs.

Everyday puzzles, explained

Questions people actually ask

Why does my phone need to be closer than a shop reader?

A phone runs on a battery and has a small ring-shaped coil squeezed around its camera, so it drives that coil gently. On this page the phone reaches a card at about —, compared with — for the shop reader. The camera bump also keeps the card about 8 mm away from the phone’s coil, even when touching.

Why does tapping sometimes fail when I hold the card at an angle?

Only the field passing through the card’s loop counts. Tilt it towards 90° and the loop’s opening turns away from the field lines. Combined with the energy cliff, a tilted card can drop below what the chip needs.

Why is a key fob harder to read than a card?

Its coil is much smaller, about 2 × 3 cm instead of 8 × 5 cm, so it catches less of the field even with more turns of wire. Less field through the loop means less energy, so it must be closer.

Could someone read my card from across the room?

Researchers have woken cards from a few tens of centimetres with large antennas and lots of power. Across a room is not practical, because the energy cliff is brutal. Listening in on a payment that is already happening is easier: then the real reader supplies the power. That is one reason contactless payments are also protected by encryption and one-time codes.

What about car keys? Mine has a battery.

The buttons on a car key use a small battery-powered radio. Many keys also hide a battery-free RFID tag, like the coil in this fob, so the car can still recognise the key when the battery is dead: you hold it against a marked spot or the start button. Car keys and older door fobs often use a slower rhythm, around 125 thousand pushes per second instead of 13.56 million, but the trick is exactly the same.

Why does it not work through metal or with two cards together?

Metal near the coil carries currents of its own that push back against the reader’s field, so very little reaches the card. Two cards stacked together pull each other’s tuning (see Tuning), and both try to answer at once. Payment terminals refuse on purpose when they hear two cards, so they never charge the wrong one. This page simulates one card in open air.

Is this the same as the security tags on clothes, or the chips in passports?

Passports, transit cards, contactless bank cards and phone payments (NFC) all use this same 13.56 MHz magnetic trick. Shop security tags and warehouse labels often use different versions: some work at much higher radio frequencies and reflect radio waves, which lets them work from metres away, and the simplest ones just ring when they sense a field. The principle stays the same: the tag does not transmit, it changes how it affects the reader’s signal.

Under the hood

How this page calculates everything

Nothing on this page is a recording. Each time you move something, the browser runs this chain of calculations, written from scratch in js/physics.js:

  1. Coil shapesCoils measured from the 3D models: card antenna 81.5 × 49.3 mm (4 turns), fob coil 29.1 × 19.5 mm (8 turns), the phone’s NFC coil, a ring 60 mm across around the camera (2 turns), and a shop reader at 66 × 66 mm (3 turns).
  2. Magnetic couplingMutual inductance M = ∮ A·dl, using the exact vector potential of each straight wire, A = μ₀I/4π · ln((r₁+r₂+L)/(r₁+r₂−L)), integrated around every turn of the tag with Gauss–Legendre quadrature. Self-inductance uses the same method.
  3. Coupled circuitsComplex numbers (phasors) at 13.56 MHz. Reader: a series resonant circuit driven by a source. Tag: coil + tuning capacitor + chip. I₁ = Vs / (Z₁ + (ωM)²/Z₂), Vchip = |jωM I₁ ZL / Z₂|. The chip’s protection clamp is solved by searching for the load that holds 4.5 V.
  4. Load modulationThe circuit is solved twice, switch open and closed (an extra resistor in parallel with the chip). The difference in the reader’s coil voltage is the reply.
  5. SignalBytes with odd parity and an XOR check byte, Manchester-coded on an 847.5 kHz subcarrier at 106 kbit/s (like ISO/IEC 14443-A), sampled at 6.78 MHz, with Gaussian hiss and a slow sinusoidal wobble added.
  6. DecoderFor each half bit: I/Q correlation with the subcarrier (or, without it, the average level), compare the two halves, rebuild bytes, run parity and check-byte tests.

What is simplified

  • Coils are perfect rectangles or rings in open air: no metal, ferrite, hands or tables nearby.
  • The chip is modelled as a resistor (1 kΩ) that wakes up at 2.0 V and is clamped at 4.5 V. Real chips draw power in more complicated ways.
  • The reader is an ideal resonant circuit with a fixed drive. Real readers adjust their power and measure in more clever ways.
  • The reader-to-card direction (the reader briefly switching its field off to send commands) is not shown. This page focuses on how the card answers.
  • Noise is idealised: random hiss plus one slow wobble.
